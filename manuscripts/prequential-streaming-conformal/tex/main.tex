Prequential Streaming Evaluation of Stateful Forecasters with Online Conformal Calibration}

\IEEEaftertitletext{\vspace{-0.6\baselineskip}\headerblock\vspace{0.2\baselineskip}}

\IEEEtitleabstractindextext{%
\begin{abstract}
Forecasting may be deployed as a stream: observations arrive one by one, the model updates, and
its prediction intervals are expected to remain useful as the series changes. We provide a
prequential streaming evaluation harness, released as
the open-source package \texttt{preqts}: it implements Dawid's predict-then-observe-then-update
protocol behind a \texttt{StatefulForecaster} interface with an explicit covariate-arrival
policy, and records four trajectories per method: rolling scaled error, rolling empirical
coverage, rolling weighted quantile loss, and cumulative measured compute time. The package
supports stateful and replay-based forecasters; the experiment reported here uses replay-based
Theta wrappers and does not compare constant-cost native state updates. The
harness is embedded in an
atlas that characterizes each series with ten diagnostic families (stationarity,
autocorrelation, seasonality, filters, change points, volatility, distribution and complexity,
fractal structure, nonlinear dynamics, and cross-series causality) and then forecasts it with a
method catalogue spanning classical, statistical, gradient-boosted, deep, and foundation-model
methods. Using the harness we test
a concrete claim: that online conformal calibration keeps streaming intervals at nominal under
distribution shift. On fifteen series that span distinct regimes and include white-noise and
random-walk negative controls, wrapping a Theta point forecaster in Adaptive Conformal Inference
or Conformal-PID control reduces the mean absolute coverage error at the nominal $80\%$ level
from $0.0416$ (raw Theta) and $0.0599$ (seasonal-naive) to $0.0057$ and $0.0060$, a seven-to-tenfold
reduction, and shrinks the across-case span of the final coverage from $0.19$--$0.40$ to $0.04$,
without changing the point forecast. Calibrated coverage is also near target on the two generated
negative controls, which provide limited checks against simple predictable-versus-unpredictable
confounds but cannot rule out all leakage. The contribution is the reusable prequential harness
and an artifact-backed comparison under the protocol defined below.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
time-series forecasting, prequential evaluation, streaming, conformal prediction, adaptive

\end{IEEEkeywords}}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

\IEEEPARstart{F}{orecasting} deployed as a stream has a sequential information constraint: at
each cutoff, a forecast must be made from information available then, scored after the target
arrives, and followed by an update. Rolling-origin evaluations are useful for other questions;
their treatment of model state depends on the evaluated method and adapter. We do not claim that
existing benchmark harnesses universally omit stateful or prequential evaluation. We instead
describe and release \texttt{preqts}, which makes the predict-score-ingest order and covariate
arrival policy explicit, and use it to evaluate an artifact-backed interval-calibration study.

The harness implements the prequential principle, Dawid's ``predictive sequential''
protocol~\cite{dawid1984}: at each step the model predicts, the truth is revealed and scored, and
only then does the model update. Metrics can therefore be inspected along the stream rather than
only as one aggregate. The harness is embedded in an
atlas (Section~\ref{sec:atlas}) that pairs forecasts with a diagnostic layer. We then use the
harness to measure
(Section~\ref{sec:results}): does online conformal calibration keep streaming intervals
near the nominal level on this selected set of changing and control series? Our contributions
are: (i) the reusable prequential streaming harness \texttt{preqts}, with a covariate-arrival
policy; and (ii) an artifact-backed comparison showing lower descriptive coverage error for
online calibration than for raw intervals in these 15 cases, while retaining the same Theta
median forecast.

% =====================================================================
\section{The atlas: understand, then forecast}
\label{sec:atlas}
% =====================================================================

ChronoScope pairs a diagnostic layer with a forecast ladder over fifteen cases, each
computed deterministically (an artifact is a pure function of case and seed). \textbf{Ten
diagnostic families} characterize a series before it is forecast: stationarity and unit roots
(ADF, KPSS, Phillips--Perron, DF-GLS, Zivot--Andrews); autocorrelation (ACF/PACF, Ljung--Box);
seasonality (periodogram, STL/MSTL, seasonal strength); filters and adaptive decompositions
(Hodrick--Prescott, Baxter--King, EMD/CEEMDAN, wavelet scalogram); change points and regimes
(PELT, OLS-CUSUM, Hamilton Markov-switching); volatility and transforms (ARCH-LM, GARCH,
Box--Cox); distribution, normality and complexity (moments, Jarque--Bera, sample/spectral
entropy, the BDS test, and the catch22 feature set~\cite{lubba2019}); fractal and multifractal
structure (Hurst via R/S and DFA, MF-DFA, ARFIMA long memory); nonlinear dynamics and chaos
(Takens embedding, largest Lyapunov exponent, recurrence quantification, the 0--1 test, with a
surrogate-data test for nonlinearity); and cross-series causality (cross-correlation lead/lag,
Granger, Engle--Granger and Johansen cointegration). Each is a verified implementation wrapped in
a NaN-safe API and precomputed per case.

\textbf{A nineteen-method forecast ladder} is then scored on the same cases: classical
numpy live methods (seasonal-naive, SES, Holt, Holt--Winters, Theta~\cite{assimakopoulos2000}),
statistical methods (AutoARIMA, AutoETS, AutoTheta), a gradient-boosted method (LightGBM), deep
methods, and stateful zero-shot foundation models (Chronos~\cite{ansari2024},
TimesFM~\cite{das2024}). The cases span distinct regimes by construction, level shift,
deterministic chaos, GARCH heteroskedasticity, intermittent demand, long memory, coupled
daily/weekly seasonality, exogenous promotions, plus real CC-BY series (UCI Electricity, Beijing
PM2.5, M4 excerpts~\cite{makridakis2020}) and two negative controls, white noise and a near-random walk. There is no
universal winner: the diagnosis explains the leaderboard, which is exactly the setting in which
leakage-free scoring matters most.

% =====================================================================
\section{The prequential streaming protocol}
\label{sec:harness}
% =====================================================================

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{fig-prequential.pdf}
\caption{The prequential test-then-train loop for a stateful forecaster. At each step the model
predicts from its current state, the truth is revealed and scored, and only then is the state
updated; a covariate-arrival policy declares, per channel, whether a value is known at prediction
time or revealed with the target. Stateless-window harnesses reset state each window and run no
test-then-train loop, so they cannot see a calibrator recover coverage after a shift.}
\label{fig:prequential}
\end{figure*}

Let the stored target series be $y_0,\ldots,y_{T-1}$, let $m$ denote its case-specific seasonal
period, and let $h$ denote the forecast horizon. In the experiment reported here $h=1$ and the
cutoff step is one observation. At cutoff $t$, the forecaster receives only the initial context
$y_{0:t-1}$ and observations subsequently revealed at earlier cutoffs. It returns quantiles at
$q\in\{0.1,0.5,0.9\}$. The prediction is recorded and scored against the next observation before
that observation is passed to the forecaster:
\begin{equation}
(\hat Q_{t,0.1},\hat Q_{t,0.5},\hat Q_{t,0.9})=f_\text{predict}(s_t),\qquad
s_{t+1}=f_\text{ingest}(s_t,y_t).
\end{equation}
The initial context length is $w=\min(\max(2m,20),\max(1,\lfloor T/3\rfloor))$. The harness
initializes each forecaster once with $y_{0:w-1}$ and evaluates cutoffs $t=w,\ldots,T-h$;
after scoring at $t$, it ingests $y_t$ and advances by one. Thus the target being scored is not
available to either the point forecaster or the interval controller when the forecast is made.
For a covariate channel, the stream contract distinguishes past-only values (optionally delayed by
their declared lag) from known-future values. The conformal experiment has no covariates, so this
policy does not affect its results.

The evaluated point method is the archived NumPy smoothing-plus-drift adaptation labelled Theta,
not a streaming-native state update or the finite-history Theta equation used in the current final-block experiment. At every cutoff, a \texttt{ReplayAdapter} calls the batch forecaster on the full
history then available. For a context $x_0,\ldots,x_{n-1}$, the implementation estimates an OLS
slope $b$ against integer time, selects the SES coefficient $a$ from
$\{0.05,0.10,\ldots,0.95\}$ by minimum in-sample one-step squared error, and updates the level
$\ell_i=a x_i+(1-a)\ell_{i-1}$ from $\ell_0=x_0$. Its one-step fitted values are the prior
levels; $\hat\sigma$ is the population standard deviation of the resulting one-step residuals.
For lead $j=1,\ldots,h$, the point path is $\hat y_{t+j}=\ell_{n-1}+0.5 b j$. The base quantiles
are $\hat Q_{t,j,q}=\hat y_{t+j}+\Phi^{-1}(q)\hat\sigma\sqrt{j}$, using the implementation's
Acklam approximation to the normal quantile. This Gaussian widening is an implementation choice,
not a distribution-free interval guarantee. In the reported one-step experiment, this reduces to
the evaluated adaptation's point forecast plus or minus $\Phi^{-1}(0.9)\hat\sigma$.

At each forecast the harness records one-step MASE, empirical coverage of the inclusive interval
$[\hat Q_{0.1},\hat Q_{0.9}]$, WQL at the three requested quantiles, and prediction and ingestion
latency. MASE divides absolute error by a single scale fixed from the warmup context,
$S_w=(T_w-m)^{-1}\sum_{i=m}^{w-1}|y_i-y_{i-m}|$ (with the package's documented fallback for a
short or constant context). WQL follows the implementation's GluonTS-style normalization:
$2\sum_q\rho_q(y-\hat Q_q)/\sum_i|y_i|$, where $\rho_q(u)=u(q-\mathbf1\{u<0\})$ is pinball
loss. Each displayed rolling trajectory is the trailing arithmetic mean over at most 25 cutoff
values, including the current cutoff. Cumulative cost is the running sum of measured prediction
and ingestion latencies; it is a machine- and run-dependent timing observation, not an asymptotic
complexity proof. The package also computes other metrics, but they are not used for the headline
plots in this work. The three-quantile WQL summary is not treated as a CRPS estimate.

The experiment uses the package API \texttt{run\_prequential} from \texttt{preqts==0.3.0};
the series, case-specific seasonal periods and generated artifacts are those committed under
\path{data/derived/}. Each method starts from the same case prefix and receives the same target
sequence. The four methods are seasonal-naive, uncalibrated Theta, Theta with ACI, and Theta with
Conformal-PID. Only the two wrappers change the interval; they retain the same Theta median
forecast. The adapters recompute each batch forecast from its accumulated history, so this
experiment compares their interval behavior under a common replay protocol and does not measure
the benefit of persistent internal model state.

\section{Interval calibration and experimental design}
\label{sec:conformal}
% =====================================================================

The wrappers recalibrate the outer interval of the same Theta forecast. For a one-step base
forecast, write its median as $m_t$ and its half-width as
$r_t=\max\{(\hat Q_{t,0.9}-\hat Q_{t,0.1})/2,10^{-8}\}$. Once $y_t$ arrives, the nonconformity
score is the scale-normalized absolute error
$z_t=|y_t-m_t|/r_t$. This is not raw-residual split conformal: it is a sequential score buffer
and a multiplicative correction to the base interval. Each horizon has a separate state in the
package; the experiment uses only horizon one. For either method, the calibrated outer endpoints
are $m_t\pm \hat q_t r_t$, where $\hat q_t$ is the empirical quantile of the previous scores in
that horizon's buffer at level $1-\alpha_t$. The buffer is a FIFO deque capped at 200 scores and
the quantile is NumPy's default linearly interpolated sample quantile. Before any score is
available, $\hat q_t=1$. The current prediction is therefore formed from past state only; its
score enters the buffer after that prediction has been scored.

\textbf{Adaptive Conformal Inference} (ACI)~\cite{gibbs2021} begins with target miscoverage
$\alpha_0=1-c=0.2$, where $c=0.8$. After the current miss indicator
$e_t=\mathbf1\{|y_t-m_t|>\hat q_t r_t\}$ is evaluated using the multiplier that generated the
forecast, it updates
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{t+1}=\operatorname{clip}\!\left(\alpha_t+\gamma\bigl((1-c)-e_t\bigr),10^{-4},1-10^{-4}\right),
\label{eq:aci}
\end{equation}
with $\gamma=0.02$. A miss reduces $\alpha_t$, raising the next quantile level and generally
expanding the interval; a covered observation increases $\alpha_t$ and generally contracts it.
Clipping bounds the effective level. These implementation details do not establish the
an unconditional convergence theorem: the empirical rolling-quantile buffer,
level clipping, and this particular base interval define the evaluated procedure, and the results
below are finite-stream measurements rather than a new coverage guarantee.

\textbf{Conformal-PID}~\cite{angelopoulos2023} uses the same score buffer and quantile rule. With
target miscoverage $\alpha^*=0.2$, its error is $u_t=\alpha^*-e_t$, its integral is
$I_t=I_{t-1}+u_t$ (initialized at zero), and its derivative is $d_t=u_t-u_{t-1}$ (initialized
to zero on the first update). The controller computes
\begin{align}
v_t &= k_pu_t+k_iI_t+k_dd_t,\\
\alpha_{t+1}&=\operatorname{clip}(\alpha^*+v_t,10^{-4},1-10^{-4}),
\end{align}
with $k_p=0.05$, $k_i=0.02$, and $k_d=0.02$. The cumulative integral itself is not separately
clipped in the implementation. These gains are hard-coded in the bake; the repository does not
record a parameter-selection or sensitivity-analysis procedure. The wrapper changes only the
outer quantiles, leaving the median column and hence the point forecast unchanged.

The comparison is a fixed artifact-based study of 15 repository cases: 11 generated regimes and
four redistributed real-data cases, with two generated cases designated as negative controls
(white noise and near-random walk). The checked-in artifacts identify the cases and preserve
their streaming metric trajectories. The current admitted corpus also contains source values for all fifteen cases. The streaming predictions and timings retain their original computation provenance; they are not regenerated by rescoring the corrected final-block comparison.
The streaming stage sets $c=0.8$, $h=1$, levels $(0.1,0.5,0.9)$, step one, and the
warmup formula in Section~III. The main figures give equal weight to each case. For case $i$ and
method $a$, let $C_{i,a,k}$ be the stored trailing-25 coverage at cutoff $k$. The plotted summary
is $\frac1{15}\sum_i|K_i^{-1}\sum_{k\in K_i}C_{i,a,k}-0.8|$; the whisker reaches the largest
case-level absolute deviation. The per-case figure instead plots each artifact's final full-run
coverage, with its across-case range. From the committed trajectories, the rolling-trajectory
summary is 0.0599 for seasonal-naive, 0.0416 for raw Theta, 0.0057 for Theta+ACI and 0.0060 for
Theta+PID; the largest case-level deviations are 0.3071, 0.1124, 0.0225 and 0.0224,
respectively. The final-coverage spans are 0.3964, 0.1912, 0.0444 and 0.0444, respectively. These
are descriptive summaries of this selected set, not inferential estimates for a population of
forecasting tasks.

The calibration result is limited in several ways. First, there are only 15 deliberately varied
cases, and the four real-data examples do not establish broad real-world generalization. Second,
the experiment evaluates a one-step horizon, one nominal coverage, one base forecaster and one
fixed parameter setting per controller; no sensitivity or repeated-seed study is present. Third,
the base interval is a Gaussian approximation using in-sample residual dispersion, and Theta is
re-fit by full-history replay at every cutoff. Fourth, the committed aggregate trajectories do not
by themselves preserve per-time forecasts, scores, controller states, or timing environment
metadata, so they permit the reported aggregate calculations but not an independent replay of
every online update from the artifact alone. Finally, finite-run calibration and the two controls
do not prove distribution-free coverage, rule out every form of leakage, or establish a general
advantage over other forecasting methods. The exact rationale for the hard-coded ACI/PID gains and
the complete source-data revision identifiers are not recorded in the streaming artifacts; those
provenance gaps remain open.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =====================================================================

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig-coverage-summary.pdf}
\caption{Mean absolute coverage error at nominal $80\%$ over the fifteen cases (bar) with the
worst-case per-case error (whisker). Online conformal cuts the error seven-to-tenfold. Data: the
per-case \texttt{streaming.json} artifacts.}
\label{fig:summary}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig-coverage-percase.pdf}
\caption{Final empirical coverage, one point per case. The calibrated methods cluster tightly at
the nominal level (across-case span $0.04$) while the raw and naive intervals spread widely (span
$0.19$ and $0.40$). Data: \texttt{streaming.json}.}
\label{fig:percase}
\end{figure}

The headline is Fig.~\ref{fig:summary}. Averaged over the fifteen cases, the raw Theta intervals
miss the nominal $80\%$ target by a mean absolute coverage error of $0.0416$ and the seasonal-naive
floor by $0.0599$, with worst-case per-case errors of $0.1124$ and $0.3071$. Wrapping the \emph{same}
Theta point forecaster in ACI or Conformal-PID cuts the mean coverage error to $0.0057$ and
$0.0060$, a sevenfold reduction over raw Theta and tenfold over seasonal-naive. The across-case
view (Fig.~\ref{fig:percase}) shows the same effect as a collapse of dispersion: the final
coverage of the calibrated methods clusters within a $0.04$ span at nominal, while raw Theta
spreads over $0.19$ and seasonal-naive over $0.40$. The point forecast is unchanged (the MASE of
Theta, Theta+ACI and Theta+PID is identical per case); only the interval is adjusted. In the selected cases, where raw intervals drift most, the calibrators pull the time-averaged coverage
back (intermittent demand from $0.91$ raw to $0.81$ with ACI; chaotic Mackey--Glass from $0.77$
raw to $0.80$; real UCI electricity from $0.76$ raw to $0.80$). On the white-noise and
near-random-walk controls, observed coverage summaries are also near nominal after calibration.
These two examples are limited checks against simple confounds; they do not establish that the
harness is free of leakage.

% =====================================================================
\section{Discussion and scope}
% =====================================================================

Online calibration is designed to adapt interval coverage, and the measurements here concern one
specific replay-based implementation and selected set of cases. The prequential protocol scores
each forecast before revealing its target, while the full-history replay makes the base Theta
forecast and its timing depend on the accumulated context. The identical median forecasts across
raw and calibrated variants isolate interval adjustment in this experiment; the negative controls
provide only two limited checks and do not rule out leakage. The atlas combines generated regimes
with four real-data cases, so its real-data coverage evidence remains narrow. Results are shown at
horizon one and nominal $80\%$; the package interface accepts other horizons and coverage levels,
but those settings are not evaluated here. This work does not propose a new calibrator or establish
a general advantage over alternative forecasting methods.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We describe \texttt{preqts}, a prequential evaluation harness with an explicit covariate-arrival
policy, and report an artifact-backed interval-calibration comparison on 15 selected cases. Under
the stated one-step protocol, the mean absolute deviation of each case's rolling coverage summary
from the nominal level is lower for ACI and Conformal-PID than for the uncalibrated Theta and
seasonal-naive intervals. The median forecast is unchanged by the wrappers. These finite-case
results do not establish distribution-free coverage or generalization to other horizons, base
forecasters, controller settings, or data populations.
